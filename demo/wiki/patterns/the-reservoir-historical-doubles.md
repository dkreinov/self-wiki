## Overview

Every recurring group of people, given enough time, begins to resemble a historical cast. The Reservoir is no exception. The matches below are not flattery — they are diagnoses, made in the spirit of understanding rather than celebration.

| Member | Historical Double | Core Parallel |
|---|---|---|
| [[Mr. White]] | Socrates | Uses questions as the primary instrument; infuriating at dinner |
| [[Mr. Pink]] | Marcus Aurelius | Obligations first, then everything else; wisdom expressed as practice |
| [[Mr. Blonde]] | Anton Chekhov | Documents ordinary sadness with a doctor's precision and a writer's mercy |
| [[Mr. Silver]] | Ursula K. Le Guin | Builds alternate worlds not to escape but to see this one more clearly |

## The Socrates Problem — Mr. White

Socrates claimed to know nothing, which was not false modesty — it was a method. By maintaining the position of the ignorant questioner, he could destabilize any certainty without defending a position of his own. [[Mr. White]] operates identically. The group has learned that his questions are not requests for information. They are structural interventions.

The Socratic parallel has one uncomfortable extension: Socrates was eventually voted out of the room. The group has threatened this precisely once, over a two-hour argument about whether a table exists when no one is looking at it.

## The Marcus Aurelius Problem — Mr. Pink

Marcus Aurelius wrote a private journal of obligations to himself that he never intended to publish. It became one of the most-read books in history. The paradox of private rigor becoming public wisdom applies to [[Mr. Pink]]: his standards are for himself, applied to himself, and their effect on others is incidental. He would say this is the point.

## The Chekhov Problem — Mr. Blonde

Chekhov was a doctor who wrote. The medicine taught him that people rarely say what is wrong with them; the writing taught him to notice what they said instead. [[Mr. Blonde]] teaches history the same way — what actually happened, versus what the participants believed was happening. The gap between the two is where he lives.

The Chekhov parallel has an uncomfortable extension that the others do not: Chekhov died young, having spent his final years watching everything he loved deteriorate while remaining too polite to say so directly. The group does not make this observation aloud.

## The Le Guin Problem — Mr. Silver

Le Guin's insight was that the best way to examine something is to remove it — strip gender from society, strip capitalism from economy, strip time from narrative, and see what remains. [[Mr. Silver]]'s illustration work operates by the same principle: he removes noise until the essential lines appear. His conversation does the same, which is why his observations arrive obliquely and stick.

Le Guin also had long silences between books. The group finds this parallel less comfortable than the others.
