## Overview

Personalized reading picks drawn from The Reservoir's shared library, matched to each member's particular way of being in the world. The goal is not to give people books they will like — they can find those themselves. The goal is to give them books that will argue with them productively.

## For Mr. White — The Questioner

[[Mr. White]] reads to destabilize certainty. He needs books that do the same thing to him that he does to everyone else.

**Gödel, Escher, Bach** by Douglas Hofstadter — A book about self-reference, strange loops, and what it means to think inside a system you cannot fully see. Mr. White has read it twice and claims it answered nothing while making the questions better.

**The Trial** by Franz Kafka — The system is real, the charges are real, and understanding neither is the point. A perfect Socratic trap: the more you interrogate it, the more it recedes.

**Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance** by Robert Pirsig — The only book Mr. White has recommended to every member of the group. A meditation on quality, rationality, and the ghost in the machine. Mr. Pink found it self-indulgent. Mr. Silver found it essential. Mr. Blonde assigned it to his students.

## For Mr. Pink — The Pragmatist

[[Mr. Pink]] reads to get better at his job, broadly defined. His job is keeping people alive and thinking clearly. He needs books with high signal and no wasted sentiment.

**The Emperor of All Maladies** by Siddhartha Mukherjee — A biography of cancer: its science, its history, and the stubbornness that drives both the disease and its treatment. Mr. Pink gave it to Mr. Blonde. He spent two weeks in quiet devastation.

**Being Mortal** by Atul Gawande — On medicine's failure to help people die well. Mr. Pink considers this required reading for anyone who will one day be ill, which is everyone.

**Meditations** by Marcus Aurelius — His most-loaned book. Private instructions to oneself on how to do the work, absorb the difficulty, and remain functional. He annotates it in red pen and loans the annotated copy.

## For Mr. Blonde — The Historian

[[Mr. Blonde]] reads to understand how catastrophe becomes ordinary. He needs books that take the long view without losing the texture of individual experience.

**Stoner** by John Williams — A novel about a man who teaches and reads and is largely overlooked by history. Group consensus: you feel accused by it. Mr. Blonde considers this the correct response.

**The Remains of the Day** by Kazuo Ishiguro — A butler who served a man who admired fascism, told with devastating restraint. The question underneath: how much of a life can you give over to an institution before it is no longer your life? Mr. Blonde has reread this three times and claims to find it less devastating each time. The group is skeptical.

**The Cherry Orchard** by Anton Chekhov — The family can see the orchard being sold. They cannot stop it. Mr. Blonde teaches this play as a model for how historical change actually moves: everyone watching, no one able to act, the sound of the axes beginning offstage.

## For Mr. Silver — The World-Builder

[[Mr. Silver]] reads science fiction as philosophy. He needs books that construct alternate realities precisely enough to illuminate this one.

**The Left Hand of Darkness** by Ursula K. Le Guin — A novel set on a world with no fixed gender. Not an argument — a thought experiment so rigorous it becomes one. Mr. Silver has re-read it six times and finds it differently useful each time.

**Invisible Cities** by Italo Calvino — Marco Polo describes cities to Kublai Khan, each a different way of organizing human desire. Structurally a prose poem, functionally a philosophy of place. Mr. Silver calls it the best book about illustration he has ever read, despite containing no illustration.

**The Book of the New Sun** by Gene Wolfe — An unreliable narrator in a dying civilization, written in a language that has forgotten what it no longer has. Mr. Silver recommended it to Mr. White; he spent a month convinced it contained a code. It might.
